# Research Reflection — November 18, 2026

*Buying Visibility, Negotiating Narratives: Rwanda, DR Congo, and the Politics of Representation in European Football*

> Researcher's evolving reflection at the transition from news discovery to source verification and analysis. These are emerging propositions and methodological decisions, **not coded findings**.

Today the project moved into a more intellectually demanding phase. After examining government announcements and official European football club communications, I expanded the third corpus—journalistic coverage—across African, European, and U.S. news outlets. The preliminary discovery registers now contain **71 candidate news items**: **37 African press records** (8 from Rwanda-based outlets, 15 from DRC-based outlets, and 14 from other African or pan-African outlets) and **34 international press records** (10 UK, 10 France, 4 Italy, 3 Germany, 1 Spain, and 6 United States). These records contain titles, publishers, URLs, event links, and preliminary metadata. They are **not yet 71 independently verified, eligible or coded articles**, and the article texts have not been systematically archived.

The expansion sharpened an observation that I want to preserve before systematic coding begins: **buying visibility is not the same as controlling its interpretation**. A government can purchase branded presence on a European club's shirt or in its commercial communications, but that agreement does not purchase favorable coverage in *The Guardian*, the BBC, *Le Monde*, *L’Équipe*, Italian newspapers, or other independent newsrooms. Sponsorship may generate editorial attention, but the stories can focus on tourism, international recognition, investment and youth development—or on conflict, human rights, public expenditure, fan opposition, disputed contracts and the credibility of promised benefits. The analytic task is to establish which of these actually occurs, how often and in which circumstances, rather than assuming that negative attention dominates.

This distinction makes the question of **visibility** more precise. There are at least three different things to measure. The first is *paid or contractually arranged brand exposure*: the space and promotional opportunities secured through sponsorship. The second is *earned journalistic attention*: whether and how news organizations cover a partnership. The third is *narrative uptake*: whether news stories reproduce, attribute, qualify, dispute or displace the government's intended messages. A sponsorship can succeed at the first while yielding ambiguous or critical results at the second and third. Conversely, critical journalism may increase public recognition of a country's name without advancing the image its sponsors seek to cultivate.

An additional, intriguing possibility is **noncoverage**. What if a government makes an expensive deal with a prestigious European club but some influential foreign news outlets barely cover it? That could be analytically significant, but only if I construct a transparent outlet-by-event search matrix, apply comparable search windows, document search terms and access restrictions, and distinguish *not found after a documented search* from *never published*. Missing a story in ordinary web searches is not evidence that the outlet ignored the event. Neither is the absence of favorable journalism proof that the sponsorship failed: a government's objectives may also include broadcast exposure, tourism awareness, elite access, domestic legitimacy, or commercial relationships beyond newspaper coverage.

The Rwanda–DR Congo comparison remains central. These neighboring states should not be flattened into a single claim about 'African image-making.' Their political circumstances, narratives and sponsorship trajectories differ. Early discovery suggests that Rwanda-based reporting often emphasizes international promotion, tourism and opportunities tied to football, while some DRC-based reporting foregrounds spending, justification and implementation. That contrast is a **hypothesis suggested by discovered titles and initial source descriptions**, not a confirmed national pattern. I need to read and code the stories, examine exceptions, and account for source selection, editorial independence, opinion genres and wire-service syndication. It is equally important to recognize that Congolese criticism may be directed at DRC sponsorship spending as well as Rwanda's agreements; African political agency includes internal disagreement and accountability, not merely competition with Europe.

The European press adds another interpretive layer. British reports on Arsenal, French coverage of PSG, Monaco and both countries' sponsorship ambitions, and Italian accounts of DRC–AC Milan arrangements may reveal how host-country football economies and national newsroom priorities shape which details become salient. The coverage of AC Milan also exposes an important gap in the club-communication register: that club's own official announcements and the status of any disputed agreements require careful verification. I should investigate reported payment disputes through original sources and attribute contested assertions rather than repeat them as established facts. A new Aston Villa-related candidate likewise requires a consistent inclusion decision.

This leads me to a working question:

> **When African governments seek international visibility through European football, whose narratives ultimately become visible, through which media institutions, and on whose terms?**

And to a deliberately testable proposition:

> **European football sponsorship can purchase branded placement, but not control over independent journalistic interpretation.**

I do not yet know whether the collected evidence will support a broader argument about insufficient favorable foreign coverage, sharply different coverage across the two countries, or the predominance of critical framing. I need to let verification and coding decide. The discovery totals are the result of a targeted search, not measures of media salience, representativeness, or sentiment.

## Decision at this research stage

I will **finish verification before analysis**. The immediate work is to check exact article titles, dates, bylines, origin, languages, genre, sponsorship relevance and access; remove duplicates and identify AFP/Reuters/AP reproductions; explicitly document exclusion decisions; establish common event windows; and assemble a complete outlet-by-event matrix that records searches yielding zero results as well as positive matches. I will then pilot a shared codebook across African and international press for prominence, framing/valence, narrative uptake, cited voices, accountability and counter-narratives, with intercoder reliability procedures where feasible.

The study retains **three empirical corpora**, not four: (1) government communications; (2) European football club communications; and (3) journalism, stratified by African and international outlets. Commercial arrangements and political economy remain analytic dimensions across these corpora, not an additional textual corpus. The candidate registers and protocols are preserved in the repository for reproducibility.

What feels most important today is that this project is no longer simply cataloguing sponsorship deals. It is becoming an examination of the limits of money, intermediaries and state agency in a transnational communication system. Visibility may be bought; meaning must still be negotiated. The next responsibility is to test that insight rigorously, including the possibility that the evidence will complicate or contradict it.
