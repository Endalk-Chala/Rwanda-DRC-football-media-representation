# Research Reflection — November 17, 2026

*Rwanda–DR Congo Football Sponsorship and Global Media Representation*

> Researcher's positional and analytical reflection. This note records emerging questions and interpretations, not coded empirical findings.

As I move from government communications to European football club communications, I am beginning to see this project as more than a study of sports sponsorship. It is increasingly becoming an inquiry into who has the resources to shape Africa's global image, how that image is negotiated, and whose interests become visible or remain obscured in the process.

The comparison between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo is especially interesting. Both governments seek international visibility through European football, but their historical circumstances, institutional approaches, and political narratives differ. Their partnerships invite questions about the relationship between commercial visibility, national reputation, development, and political legitimacy.

What strikes me most is the position of European football clubs as intermediaries. They are not simply passive platforms carrying government messages. They have their own commercial interests, institutional reputations, audiences, and responsibilities. The changes in Bayern Munich's relationship with Rwanda, for example, suggest that sponsorship arrangements can become sites where competing narratives and pressures intersect.

The research also raises a broader question about power. African governments are not merely subjects of representations produced elsewhere; they actively invest in influencing international perceptions. Yet their capacity to shape those perceptions operates through institutions and media systems that they do not fully control.

At this stage, I have assembled 39 government-source candidates and 19 European club communication records. These are discovery datasets, not completed analytical samples. The next stage—examining African and European journalism—will be essential for understanding whether official narratives are reproduced, challenged, contextualized, or transformed.

My central concern is to avoid treating visibility as influence, institutional claims as demonstrated outcomes, or sponsorship as inherently successful or unsuccessful. The objective is to understand how narratives travel across states, football institutions, and journalism, and what those movements reveal about the unequal but evolving politics of representing Africa in global public communication.

This is the question I want the research to answer—not simply who sponsored which club, but **who gets to tell Africa's story, through which institutions, under what conditions, and with what consequences.**
